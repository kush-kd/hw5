3 · What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why

Honestly: all three tickets, at this shop's size. The evidence from the audit trail:

  • Ticket 102 is a five-step checklist. Those steps are get_ticket → get_lease_due(1) → get_cash_position → request_payment(rent, 1) → set_ticket_status. One agent would do it in about 5 calls and 4–5 turns. The team took 13 MCP calls and 12 turns, because Facilities and Accounting each re-read the same ticket and lease.
  • Specialists start every delegation with an empty memory. Across the three tickets, get_ticket ran 10 times and get_cash_position 7 times. A single agent reads them once and keeps them in context. Accounting even re-read ticket 103 and the cash position when the Boss called it a second time.
  • Handoffs lose detail, and that caused both customer-facing errors. "5 business days" in #101 and "the remaining 12 at the same proposed price" in #103 were both written by Customer Service working from the Boss's summary, not the raw facts. One agent that looked up the lead time itself would have quoted 5 calendar days.
  • Every delegation costs overhead turns. There were 12 final_result calls, one for each agent finishing, plus 9 delegations. Those are 21 of the 78 tool calls spent on coordination rather than shop work.

Why the safety wouldn't suffer: the important rules live in the MCP tools, not in the agent boundaries.

  • human_approve_and_pay needs the human key and refuses anything that would make cash negative.
  • create_purchase_order refuses a blocked vendor.
  • request_payment takes the amount from the database.

One agent with the same 17 agent-facing tools would be just as safe with money.

What the team did buy us, and where it would start to pay off:

  • Least privilege: Customer Service physically can't call request_payment, and only the Boss can set_ticket_status.
  • Separation of duties on #102: Facilities verifies the notice before Accounting prepares the payment, like a real "two people touch every payment" control.
  • Readable audit: the dashboard can show who did what.

Those matter more as the shop grows: more tickets at once, more tools than one prompt can hold, or different people owning different budgets. For 3 tickets, 19 tools and one $3,400 account, I'd ship one agent with tools and keep the separation of duties as tool-level rules. For example, request_payment(kind=rent) could require a Facilities verification note on the ticket first.

Together, these three gaps sum up this app: the agents are excellent at deciding and preparing within one frozen day, and the human approval gate is solid. A real shop also needs money coming in, time moving forward, and conversations that actually get sent and answered.
